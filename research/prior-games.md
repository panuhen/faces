# Prior games and studies

Earlier games and trainings that teach children, mostly autistic children, to recognise or make facial expressions, what they found, and what this game takes from them. Numbers in brackets refer to the [references](#references). Bibliographic details were checked against Crossref, PubMed, the publishers' pages or the full texts in November 2026.

## Studies

| Study | What it is | What it found |
|---|---|---|
| **JEMImE** [1] | The closest match to this game. The child sees a social situation and makes the matching face (happy, angry, sad), with live gauges as feedback. Four steps: imitate without context, imitate with context, make the face on request without context, then with context. | 23 autistic children aged 6 to 12, one session of about 75 minutes. Success rose over the session. Observers rated 94% of the children as satisfied with the game; about 60% of the children called themselves completely satisfied. Sadness was produced significantly worse than happiness and anger. The authors note that the children "learned how to adapt their production to fit with the expectation of the algorithm" and could not conclude that the quality of their expressions improved. |
| **Serious games review** [2] | Review of serious games that teach social interaction and emotions to autistic people. | 31 games: 16 on emotion recognition or production, 15 on social skills. Playability was often forgotten and personalisation was limited; the game design was usually not described. Of the 16 emotion games, only 4 trained producing expressions (CopyMe, LIFEisGAME, SmileMaze, and a game by Park and colleagues), as the same group notes in [1]. |
| **LIFEisGAME** [3] | iPad game about recognising facial expressions and building them on a cartoon character (drawing or assembling a face; no camera). | 11 children aged 5 to 15, one 15-minute session. They enjoyed it, but it needed simplifying. Fear, disgust and surprise were the hardest to recognise. Suggestions included music and picture-based instructions. |
| **CopyMe** [4], [5] | iPad game in which children copy facial expressions and get real-time feedback from expression recognition. | An early example of the copy-the-face mechanic with live feedback. In a pilot at a childcare centre, the children, including autistic children, played well and generally enjoyed it [4]. |
| **MODU** [6] | Story-based game for practising empathy expressions, for children with mild intellectual disability or borderline intellectual functioning. | The format closest to this game's narrator-and-story design. Exploratory: a user study with one pair of siblings. |
| **The Transporters** [7] | Animated vehicles with real human faces, watched daily. | 20 autistic children aged 4 to 7 watched every day for 4 weeks and afterwards performed comparably to typically developing children on emotion recognition, at three levels of generalisation, including situations not shown in the series. |
| **Let's Face It!** [8] | Seven computer games for face **identity** recognition (not emotion), 20 hours of play, randomised trial. | Reliable but narrow gains: recognising mouth features, and recognising a face from its eyes. |
| **Superpower Glass** [9] | Google Glass recognises the expressions of people around the child, used at home, on top of standard therapy. Randomised trial. | 71 children aged 6 to 12, 20-minute sessions four times a week for 6 weeks. The glass group improved more on Vineland socialisation than children with standard therapy alone; the extra gain was comparable in size to gains seen with standard therapy. Three other primary measures were not significant after correction. |
| **Meta-analysis** [10] | 29 facial emotion recognition training studies, ages 4 to 18, autistic and non-autistic groups. | Improvement in both groups (standardised mean difference 0.68 for autistic and 0.77 for non-autistic participants; the latter with a very wide confidence interval). Few studies tested whether the gains last. |

The research is mostly on autistic children. MODU [6] and the meta-analysis [10] are the only sources here that also cover other groups.

## What this game takes from them

1. **Recognise first, then make the face.** The child taps a face card (the recognition skill, which can be checked reliably), then makes the face (play, with a forgiving detector). A weak detector never blocks a child who understood the story. This also follows from the JEMImE caveat [1]: a producing score is easy to game and does not show learning.
2. **One added difficulty per level**, after JEMImE's four steps [1]: copy the character's face, then the story where the character shows the feeling, then the story with a calm face, then subtler faces.
3. **The same place with different outcomes**, like JEMImE's positive and negative version of each scene [1], so children cannot learn shortcuts like "balloon means happy". The red balloon scene is happy; the next scene in the same park is sad.
4. **Grown-ups set the controls**: sensitivity, hold time, time limit, number of cards and which emotions are on. JEMImE let adults set the number of presentations, the successes needed, the recognition threshold and the time limit [1].
5. **Sad needs a forgiving check.** Sadness was the hardest expression to produce in JEMImE [1], and the face tracker's frown signal is weak (see [face-tracking.md](face-tracking.md)), so the sad check accepts alternatives.
6. **Personalisation and playability.** The review [2] found these often missing; this game has two characters, a story, stickers, and adjustable settings.
7. **Simple instructions with pictures and sound.** LIFEisGAME needed simplifying, and music and picture-based instructions were suggested [3]: hints show the character's face, and every line is read aloud.

## Not done yet

- **Real human faces.** The Transporters used real faces [7] because cartoon expressions do not necessarily carry over to people. Level 5 (real human faces) is future work.
- **Evidence of learning.** A rising score in the game does not prove learning [1]. Evidence would need a test outside the game, for example recognising emotions in photos before and after a period of play, and a check that gains last [10].
- **Masking criticism.** Many autistic adults object to tools that train children to produce typical expressions on cue. The game keeps the goal as recognising emotions in others, treats making the face as play, and never marks the child's natural expression as wrong. The design should still be reviewed by autistic adults, not only by therapists.
